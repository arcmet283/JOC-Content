TOP STORY THIS WEEK:
Steve "Air" McNair — Alcorn State University

Few HBCU stories carry the full arc that Steve McNair's does — and yet the HBCU chapter of his life is almost never the focus. Recruited by the University of Florida as a running back, McNair turned down the Gators to play quarterback at Alcorn State University in Lorman, Mississippi — a small SWAC school with no national profile and limited scouting exposure. What happened next is staggering: over four seasons (1991–1994), McNair accumulated 16,823 total yards — still an FCS record — including 14,496 passing yards, 119 touchdown passes, 2,327 rushing yards, and 33 rushing scores. His 1994 senior season alone produced 5,377 passing yards, 47 touchdowns, and 904 rushing yards, earning him the Walter Payton Award (top FCS player in the country) and a historic third-place Heisman Trophy finish — the first time an HBCU player had ever been invited to the Heisman ceremony. The Houston Oilers selected him 3rd overall in the 1995 NFL Draft, making him the highest-drafted HBCU offensive player in history; he went on to a 14-year NFL career that included a Super Bowl appearance with the Titans (Super Bowl XXXIV, the famous "one yard short" game), a co-MVP award with Peyton Manning in 2003, and three Pro Bowl selections. He was tragically shot and killed on July 4, 2009. Netflix's "Untold: The Murder of Air McNair" premiered in August 2024 and renewed broad public interest in his life — but the documentary focuses on his death, not his HBCU origins. That gap is JOC's opening.

Hook angle: "What if I told you the University of Florida recruited Steve McNair — but only to play running back? He turned them down, went to a small HBCU in Mississippi that nobody was watching, and became the third pick in the NFL Draft."

Audience this serves: 35–54 (lived through McNair's NFL career with the Titans); also captures Titans/Ravens fanbase search traffic and the Netflix documentary audience searching for more on his story
Title format suggestion: Classic Era — frame as "The HBCU QB Who Changed the NFL" or "He Turned Down Florida. He Became the #3 Pick."

4. "58 Touchdowns in One Season: The HBCU Offense That Broke Football" — Jerry Rice & Willie Totten / Mississippi Valley State — Classic Era
Story: In 1984, Mississippi Valley State's no-huddle "Satellite Express" offense — quarterback Willie Totten and wide receiver Jerry Rice under Coach Archie Cooley — shattered more than 50 NCAA Division I-AA passing records in a single season. Totten threw 58 touchdown passes. Rice caught 103 passes for 1,682 yards and 27 touchdowns, breaking his own records from 1983 and finishing 9th in Heisman Trophy voting (the best finish by an HBCU player until McNair in 1994). The stadium at MVSU is now named Rice–Totten Stadium in their honor. Rice became the greatest receiver in NFL history; Totten, after brief NFL/CFL stints, returned to MVSU as a coach. The story behind the "Satellite Express" is pure HBCU magic — two Hall of Famers, one system, one unforgettable season that the national sports world largely ignored.
Why this works for JOC: Jerry Rice is one of the most searched names in football history; this is his origin story told from the HBCU angle, and the "Coach Cooley's crazy offense" subplot is a documentary filmmaker's dream.
Sources:

5. "Before Patrick Mahomes, There Was Doug Williams: The HBCU QB Who Proved Black Men Could Win Super Bowls" — Doug Williams / Grambling State — Classic Era
Story: Before Doug Williams became the first Black quarterback to start and win a Super Bowl (Washington, Super Bowl XXII, 1988), he spent four seasons at Grambling State under legend Eddie Robinson — going 36–7 as a starter, winning three SWAC championships, and finishing 4th in Heisman Trophy voting in 1977 after throwing for 3,286 yards and 38 touchdowns. The Tampa Bay Buccaneers took him 17th overall in 1978, but paid him $120,000 per year — the lowest salary of any starting quarterback in the NFL while he was also the only Black starting QB in the league. Despite the pay inequity and documented racism from fans and ownership, Williams led Tampa Bay from 5–11 to 10–6 and a division title in year two. His Super Bowl MVP performance in 1988 — four TD passes in a single quarter, playing through a hyperextended knee — remains the defining HBCU moment in NFL championship history.
Why this works for JOC: The comparison title writes itself ("The HBCU QB Who Opened the Door for Every Black Quarterback in NFL History"), and the pay equity and racism subplot gives the story the systemic weight that turns viewers into subscribers.

STANDALONE VIDEO IDEAS:

Idea A: "The Day Black College Football Was Born: Played in Five Inches of Snow on a Cow Pasture"
Angle: On December 27, 1892, Biddle Institute (now Johnson C. Smith University) traveled 30 miles to face Livingstone College in Salisbury, North Carolina — on a converted cow pasture, in five inches of snow, with uniforms sewn by the women of Biddle's industrial department and a single regulation football the two teams pooled money to buy. Biddle won 4–0 in a contested game, and Black college football was born. The game predates the forward pass, resembles rugby more than modern football, and has been largely forgotten outside of CIAA circles. A plaque was installed at Livingstone's front campus in 1956, and the rivalry (now called the Commemorative Classic) continues today. No YouTube channel has ever told this story as a mini-documentary.
Search hook: "first HBCU football game," "history of Black college football," "oldest HBCU rivalry," "1892 HBCU football," "birthplace of Black college football"
Audience: 35–54 (history-driven); also reaches general college football history audience and HBCU pride viewers
Sources:

- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1892_Biddle_vs._Livingstone_football_game

Idea B: "60,000 Fans. No ESPN. The HBCU Classic That Outdraws Power 5 Games Nobody's Talking About"
Angle: The Magic City Classic — Alabama State vs. Alabama A&M, played annually in Birmingham at Protective Stadium (formerly Legion Field) — regularly draws 60,000–70,000 fans, making it one of the highest-attended non-Power 4 college football games in America. The Classic is as much a cultural festival as a game: a week of events, alumni reunions, band battles, and Black business showcases that generate tens of millions of dollars for the Birmingham economy. Many attendees have never watched a full game — they come for the tradition. Yet the Magic City Classic gets almost no national sports media coverage. The standalone video would examine why this game matters, the history behind the rivalry, and what it says about how HBCU culture is measured by mainstream sports.
Search hook: "Magic City Classic," "biggest HBCU game," "Alabama State vs Alabama A&M," "HBCU football attendance," "HBCU classic games"
Audience: 25–54; also captures general football fans searching for "biggest college football crowds"
Source: https://www.birminghamtimes.com/2024/10/in-honor-of-the-magic-city-classic-we-celebrate-the-hbcus-10-greatest-football-dynasties/
