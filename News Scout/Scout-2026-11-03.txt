TOP STORY THIS WEEK:
Kam Sallis — Jackson State Tigers (SWAC) / formerly Kansas State Wildcats (Big 12)

Kam Sallis began his college career at Kansas State in the Big 12, where he played primarily on special teams with limited defensive snaps as a freshman. Seeking a real role, he entered the transfer portal and signed with Jackson State on February 5, 2025 — arriving on a T.C. Taylor roster that had just won the SWAC East. In his first season with the Tigers, Sallis transformed into one of the SWAC's most complete defenders: 53 total tackles (4th on the team), 2 interceptions, 2 forced fumbles, and 2 pass breakups across 11 games, including a career-high 12 tackles against Prairie View A&M. He earned All-SWAC Second Team honors. When he entered the transfer portal in December 2025, Power 4 programs came calling. He withdrew his name in January 2026 — citing closeness to home, with his native Pickens, Mississippi just 50 miles north of Jackson — and returned to the Tigers. Entering the 2026 season, he is the preseason SWAC Defensive Player of the Year. The turning point: a player who left one of the country's biggest programs because he wasn't getting his shot, found his game at an HBCU, got so dominant the Power 4 came back for him, and chose his HBCU anyway.

Hook angle: He walked away from Kansas State's Big 12 roster to prove himself at an HBCU. He became one of the best defenders in Black college football. The Power 4 wanted him back. He said no.
Audience this serves: 25-44 (current HBCU football fans), 35-54 (SWAC loyalty audience), plus Kansas State/Big 12 search traffic from fans who don't yet know The JOC
Title format suggestion: Transfer — "He Left the Big 12 for Jackson State. The Power 4 Wanted Him Back. He Said No." or "From Kansas State to the SWAC — And He Keeps Choosing His HBCU"

He went undrafted in the 2025 NFL Draft despite that production. For the third time since 2020, no HBCU player was selected in the 2026 NFL Draft; Williams is the clearest counter-narrative.

3. "The HBCU Linebacker Who Became the Best in the NFL — Then Lost Everything to Injury" — Shaquille Leonard / South Carolina State [Draft/Advocacy — Current Era]
Story: Shaquille Leonard grew up in Lake View, South Carolina — a town so small it didn't have a stoplight — and became the greatest linebacker South Carolina State ever produced, setting the school record with 393 career tackles and winning the MEAC Defensive Player of the Year award twice (2016, 2017). NFL analysts projected him as a mid-third-round pick; the Indianapolis Colts drafted him 36th overall in 2018 — 46 spots ahead of his projection. Leonard immediately proved the analysts wrong: 2018 NFL Defensive Rookie of the Year, then First-Team All-Pro in 2018, 2019, and 2021. At his peak he was arguably the best linebacker in football. Then the injuries started: ankle, back, shoulder. He missed significant time from 2022 through 2025, returned repeatedly, but could not recapture his elite form. He announced his retirement on October 1, 2025 at age 30. The arc — tiny town to HBCU record-setter to overlooked by analysts to NFL elite to injuries to retirement — is a complete documentary story.
Why this works for JOC: The full "What Happened to Shaquille Leonard" arc is exactly the JOC format — underdog narrative, HBCU draft undervaluation, peak excellence, tragic injury — with every chapter intact; SC State/MEAC brings a loyal audience; Indianapolis Colts search traffic adds reach; retirement in October 2025 makes the full story now ready to tell.

4. "The First Player From an All-Black College Drafted #1 Overall — And Nobody Knows His Name" — Buck Buchanan / Grambling State [Classic Era — Pre-2000]
Story: Junious "Buck" Buchanan arrived at Grambling State on a scholarship arranged by legendary coach Eddie Robinson after being tipped off by Buchanan's uncle. He became a two-way player and small-college All-American. On December 1, 1962, the Dallas Texans selected Buchanan with the first overall pick in the 1963 AFL Draft — making him the first player from an all-Black college ever taken #1 in a professional football draft, more than a decade before Walter Payton went fourth overall from Jackson State. The NFL's Giants also drafted him in a later round (some sources list Green Bay — verify before scripting). He played for the Kansas City Chiefs from 1963 to 1975 under Hank Stram, made six Pro Bowls, and was elected to the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1990. He died in 1992 at age 51. His name is virtually unknown outside HBCU circles.
Why this works for JOC: The 35-54 audience responds deeply to this era of HBCU history; Buck Buchanan's story is an almost entirely untold chapter of Black college football history; Eddie Robinson and Grambling's brand add prestige; the AFL #1 pick narrative is a first-of-its-kind hook that works across football and HBCU audiences.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buck_Buchanan / https://www.profootballhof.com/players/junious-buchanan / https://hbcufootball.org/

5. "The Human Highlight Film Just Became an HBCU General Manager" — Dominique Wilkins / Alabama State Basketball [Current Era — Standalone Crossover]
Story: On October 27, 2026, Alabama State University introduced NBA Hall of Famer Dominique Wilkins as the first-ever General Manager of Hornets Basketball — a newly created role. Wilkins, a 9x All-Star, 1-time scoring champion, and "Human Highlight Film" known for his legendary 1988 Dunk Contest battle with Michael Jordan, will oversee fundraising, donor relations, NIL partnerships, revenue sharing, roster management, and player development. He called Alabama State "a new challenge," saying the program gave him an opportunity to do something different. His arrival is the highest-profile executive hire in HBCU basketball history and signals a broader shift: HBCU athletic programs are beginning to attract the star power once reserved for Power 4 schools.
Why this works for JOC: Dominique Wilkins is a universally known name bringing Atlanta Hawks, NBA, and 1980s basketball search traffic to an HBCU story; the story has no equivalent in the backlog; it bridges sports history and current HBCU institutional momentum.
