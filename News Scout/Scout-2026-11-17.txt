TOP STORY THIS WEEK:
ALVIN "SHINE" WYATT - Bethune-Cookman University

Alvin "Shine" Wyatt played defensive back at Bethune-Cookman in the late 1960s, was drafted by the Oakland Raiders in the 6th round of the 1970 NFL Draft, and spent five seasons playing in the NFL and the now-defunct World Football League. He returned to B-CU as women's basketball head coach in 1978, coaching the program for 18 seasons (245-201 record) before taking over football in 1997. He invented the "Wyattbone" triple-option offense, led Bethune-Cookman to its first outright MEAC title in 2002, a co-title in 2003, and two NCAA Division I playoff appearances, compiling a 90-54 record over 13 seasons — the most dominant run in B-CU football history. Then, two days after a 42-6 Florida Classic loss to FAMU in November 2009, Bethune-Cookman fired him at age 65 — no warning, no transition. Wyatt sued for wrongful termination, breach of contract, and ageism; a Florida state court awarded him $771,000 in damages. He went on to coach at Edward Waters from 2013-2017 and was inducted into the MEAC Hall of Fame in 2019. Now, seventeen years after being fired at the Florida Classic, Bethune-Cookman has named Wyatt interim head coach for the 2026 Florida Classic — the same game, the same field, the same rivalry — on Saturday, November 21 at Camping World Stadium in Orlando. The story of a man who built a program, was betrayed by it, won in court, and was asked to come back when no one else could fix it is a full documentary in a single career arc.

Hook angle: Open on the moment: November 23, 2009. Two days after a 42-6 humiliation in the Florida Classic, Wyatt's phone rings. He's fired. No warnings. No transition. He'd been there 30 years. Then cut to now — Week 11, 2026. The phone rings again. This time, they need him back. The Florida Classic is Saturday.
Audience this serves: 35-54 (the generation who watched him coach, and who know what a wrongful termination lawsuit at 65 means), also 25-44 (comeback narrative, systemic treatment of Black coaches, advocacy)
Title format suggestion: Comeback / Advocacy — "They Fired Him at the Florida Classic. 17 Years Later, He's Back." OR "He Built Bethune-Cookman. Then They Fired Him. Then They Called Again." OR "The Coach Who Had to Sue to Get His Respect — And Is Now Walking Back Into the Game That Ended Him"

2. "The Jackson State Linebacker Drafted Ahead of Walter Payton — Then Waited Decades for the Hall of Fame" — Robert "Dr. Doom" Brazile / Jackson State (Classic Era — Pre-2000)
   Story: Robert Brazile played linebacker at Jackson State from 1971-1974, setting a school record with 129 solo tackles and 79 assists in his senior year — the same year his teammate Walter Payton was becoming a legend at running back. The Houston Oilers drafted Brazile 6th overall in the 1975 NFL Draft, two picks before Payton went 4th to Chicago. Brazile won NFL Defensive Rookie of the Year, made seven Pro Bowls (1976-1981), recorded an estimated 48 sacks and 1,100+ tackles in 10 seasons, never missed a game (147 consecutive starts), and helped pioneer the 3-4 defense. He became known as "Dr. Doom" for his ability to terrorize opposing quarterbacks. He was not inducted into the Pro Football Hall of Fame until 2018 — more than 35 years after his final game — in one of the most overdue Hall of Fame inductions of the modern era.

3. "The Doug Williams Before Doug Williams: How James 'Shack' Harris Changed the HBCU Quarterback Story First" — James Harris / Grambling State (Classic Era — Comparison — Pre-2000)
   Story: Before Doug Williams won the Super Bowl, before Eldridge Dickey was drafted in the first round, there was James "Shack" Harris — Grambling State's greatest quarterback. Harris led Grambling to four consecutive SWAC championships in the late 1960s, was drafted by the Buffalo Bills in the 7th-8th round of the 1969 NFL Draft, and became the first Black quarterback in AFL/NFL history to start a regular-season opener (1969). He went on to become the first Black quarterback to start and win an NFL playoff game, and in January 1975, he became the first quarterback from an HBCU ever to start and win a Pro Bowl — earning Pro Bowl MVP honors. His story precedes Doug Williams' Super Bowl win by 14 years, precedes Eldridge Dickey's No. 1 draft pick story by a year, and is almost entirely unknown outside HBCU circles. In 2023, Grambling renamed its stadium field "The James 'Shack' Harris and Doug Williams Field at Eddie G. Robinson Memorial Stadium."
   Why this works for JOC: "Comparison format" — framing Harris as "The Doug Williams Before Doug Williams" directly pulls Doug Williams search traffic; the Grambling/Eddie Robinson brand adds prestige; the story fills the gap between Dickey (1968) and Williams (1988) in the history of the Black QB; serves 35-54 audience.
