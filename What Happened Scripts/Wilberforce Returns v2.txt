So, what if I told you that before Grambling became Grambling, before Jackson State became Jackson State, before any of the HBCU football programs we talk about today had built their names — there was a school in Ohio that was already dominating Black college football? And what if I told you that same program has been gone for over 80 years, and they're trying to bring it back? Well, that's Wilberforce University. And this story has layers.

Wilberforce is not a name most people know today. But it should be. Founded in 1856, Wilberforce University is the oldest private HBCU in America. And in the early 1900s, when Black athletes had almost nowhere else to play, Wilberforce was one of the schools that proved Black college football could be something special. This wasn't just about wins and losses. In an era where Black men couldn't play at most universities in this country, Wilberforce gave them a stage. And they took full advantage of it.

And then 1931 came. And that was the year everything peaked.

Coach Harry C. Graves led the Green Wave to a perfect 8-0 season. They outscored their opponents 201 to 30. Two hundred and one points. And the moment that put Wilberforce on top of the entire Black college football world came on October 24, 1931. Mills Stadium. Chicago, Illinois.

Wilberforce versus Tuskegee. Now understand what Tuskegee was at that point. They hadn't lost in 35 straight games. Thirty-five. They were untouchable — the best team in Black college football by a wide margin. And Wilberforce walked into that stadium and beat them 15-6.

Black college national champions.

Over 14,000 fans showed up to watch. Think about that for a second. Fourteen thousand people. In 1931. In the middle of the Great Depression, when families could barely put food on the table, fourteen thousand fans came to a stadium in Chicago because this program meant that much to the community. If you've got grandparents or great-grandparents who grew up in the Chicago area or the Midwest, there's a real chance somebody in your family knew about that game. Maybe somebody was in those stands.

That's how significant this was.

By 1944, World War II had drained enrollment, drained resources, and drained everything Wilberforce had built. The football program shut down. And unlike most programs that take a few years off and come back — Wilberforce never did. Over 80 years went by. No Green Wave football. Just a championship that most people forgot existed and a history that got buried under time.

But here's where the story gets interesting again.

Wilberforce launched a feasibility study to bring football back. This wasn't just a press release — enrollment is climbing toward 1,000 students, the school is growing, and the leadership believes football is the thing that could put Wilberforce back on the map in a real way. Bringing in alumni dollars, school spirit, and reconnecting the university to the broader HBCU sports world.

And then they made a move that showed they're serious. Wilberforce added women's flag football as an official varsity sport. That might not sound like a headline on its own — but think about what it means. You don't add a sport if you're not building something. That's a program growing its athletic department brick by brick. And the target for full football is fall 2027 or 2028.

They still have a mountain to climb. Facilities, funding, recruiting, compliance — you're starting a program from scratch after 80 years away. There are no shortcuts to that. But the fact that Wilberforce is taking real steps toward this, not just talking about it — that means something.

Because here's what a lot of people don't understand. When an HBCU brings back a sport, it's never just about the sport. It's about identity. It's about what that school says to its students, its alumni, and the world when it decides it's ready to compete again. Wilberforce was once the top of the mountain. They walked into Mills Stadium in 1931 and knocked off the best team in the country. They know what that feels like. And they want it back.

The question isn't whether Wilberforce has the history to deserve a football program. They proved that in front of 14,000 people in Chicago over 80 years ago. The question is whether they can build the foundation to sustain one going forward.
