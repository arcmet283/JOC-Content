JOC DOCUMENTARY SCRIPT
"The Golden Age They Took From Us"
The PS2 Era, HBCU Representation, and How College Sports Gaming Lost Its Soul

Word count: ~2,200 words | Estimated run time: 14-16 minutes
B-roll: NCAA Next gameplay throughout — HBCU team select screens, stadiums, gameplay.
Use PS2-era screenshots and box art for historical sections. EA CF25/26 menus for modern contrast.

---

Let me paint you a picture.

It's a Saturday afternoon somewhere in the early 2000s.

You've got the TV on. PlayStation 2 is fired up. And you scroll through the team select screen of NCAA Football — past Alabama, past Ohio State, past Florida — and you land on Grambling State.

You pick them.

You walk into their stadium. You hear their fight song. You run their offense. And you take a program with one of the greatest coaching legacies in college football history — and you go to work.

Now flip to a different afternoon. Same era. Same couch. This time it's basketball. You pop in College Hoops 2K. You scroll through the rosters and there's Howard University. There's North Carolina A&T. There's Florida A&M. Every Division I school, right there in the game.

You could build a dynasty with them. You could recruit. You could compete for championships. You could put an HBCU on top of the college sports world and feel every second of it.

That world existed.

And then — piece by piece — it was taken away.

This is the story of the golden age of college sports gaming. What we had, what we lost, and what it means that a free mod built by volunteers keeps that era alive better than a billion-dollar corporation ever will.

---

To understand what made the PlayStation 2 era special, you have to understand what college sports gaming looked like across the board.

This wasn't just one game. This was an ecosystem.

EA Sports had NCAA Football. Every year, updated rosters, updated stadiums, an ever-expanding world of college football. And from the very beginning, they were building toward something bigger — adding more schools, more conferences, more of the full picture of college football.

At the same time, EA had NCAA March Madness — a college basketball series that ran alongside their football franchise. Every school. Real arenas. Tournament mode. The full bracket.

And then there was the 2K Sports side of the house. College Hoops 2K. A series that by 2007 and 2008 included every single Division I basketball program in the country.

Think about what that means.

In the PlayStation 2 era, you could load up an NCAA football game and play as Grambling State, Jackson State, Florida A&M, Howard — all seventeen HBCU programs that competed in the FCS — as fully represented, playable teams.

And then you could put down the football game, pick up the basketball game, and do the same thing on the hardwood.

The same schools. The same traditions. The same culture.

Two games. Full representation. Every fall and winter, HBCUs existed in the digital landscape of college sports as first-class programs you could play as, compete with, and build legacies around.

That is the golden age.

That is what we are measuring everything against.

---

Now let me tell you how it started to end.

In 2006, Sony launched the PlayStation 3. Microsoft had already dropped the Xbox 360. The new generation had arrived.

And EA Sports made a decision.

On the old PS2 hardware, they kept everything. NCAA Football on PlayStation 2 still had all the FCS teams — every HBCU, all one hundred and twenty-plus smaller programs, the complete picture of college football.

But on PS3 and Xbox 360?

They cut them.

No Grambling. No FAMU. No Howard. No Jackson State. The entire FCS — erased from the next generation.

EA said it was a technical and licensing challenge. Getting contracts for all those smaller schools, they said, wasn't feasible for the new platforms. The development resources weren't there.

Their solution? TeamBuilder. A website where fans could design their own custom teams and download them into the game.

In other words: if you want your school, do it yourself.

That wasn't a solution. That was a dismissal.

---

For a few more years, the PS2 version kept the dream alive.

NCAA Football continued releasing on PlayStation 2 through 2011. The old hardware. The old platforms. And on those platforms, every HBCU was still there.

But the writing was on the wall.

The industry had moved on. The audience was migrating to PS3 and Xbox 360. And every year that passed, the PS2 version became more of a footnote — a legacy product for people holding on to an older machine — while the flagship versions on the new consoles had already amputated an entire level of college football.

NCAA Football 11 on PlayStation 2 was the last stand. The last time an officially licensed college football video game had HBCU programs in it.

Not the last time in the PS2 era. The last time in any era.

---

On the basketball side, the story ended differently but arrived at the same place.

College Hoops 2K8 — released in 2007 — included essentially every Division I basketball program in the country. HBCUs were in there. North Carolina A&T. Howard. Grambling's basketball program. The full landscape of college hoops.

But by 2009, 2K Sports killed the series.

The college basketball gaming market had collapsed under the weight of competition and declining sales. EA's NCAA March Madness series had struggled too. One by one, the college sports franchises started disappearing.

Football held on longer. But by 2013, the Ed O'Bannon lawsuit changed everything.

O'Bannon — a former UCLA basketball star — sued EA Sports for using the likenesses of college athletes without compensation. The case grew into something that threatened the entire NCAA licensing model. EA saw what was coming. In 2013, they announced that NCAA Football 14 would be the last game in the series.

The series was done. The franchise was over. And with it, the last remaining official connection between HBCUs and college sports gaming died completely.

---

Let me give you the timeline, because I need you to feel the weight of it.

In 2000, you could play as Grambling in NCAA Football.
In 2006, you could play as Jackson State in NCAA Football and Howard in College Hoops 2K.
In 2011, you played your last official game as an HBCU in any EA sports title.
In 2013, the series ended entirely.

From 2013 to 2024 — eleven years — there was no college football video game.

Not a bad one. Not a limited one. None.

An entire generation of college football fans came of age during those eleven years without ever being able to play a college football video game. Without ever picking up a controller and choosing their school.

And for HBCU fans, the drought started even earlier. Their programs had been gone since 2011. Thirteen years of absence, not eleven.

---

In July 2024, EA Sports College Football 25 launched.

The comeback. The return. The game everyone had been waiting for.

Hundreds of real athletes. Real likenesses. NIL deals. Modern Dynasty mode. Every major FBS school represented.

And not a single HBCU.

EA confirmed it before launch. The 128 FCS programs — the entire subdivision — would not be included. Only the 134 FBS schools made the cut.

The reason given? Licensing complexity. Getting individual agreements from all FCS programs, each with their own athletic departments, their own NIL structures, their own timelines — EA said it wasn't feasible for launch.

In 2026, College Football 26 dropped.

Same result.

Michael Vick had just taken over as head coach at Norfolk State. DeSean Jackson was at Delaware State. HBCU football was getting more mainstream coverage than it had seen in years. The stories were everywhere.

And for the second straight year, none of those programs were in the game.

EA's answer, once again, was TeamBuilder. Build your own HBCU team. Upload it. Download it. Play with it in offline modes.

The same workaround they offered in 2007.

Nearly twenty years later. The same answer.

---

Here is what I need you to sit with.

In the year 2000 — on PlayStation 2 hardware, with the technology of that era — EA Sports found a way to put Grambling State, Jackson State, Florida A&M, and fourteen other HBCU football programs in a video game.

In 2024 — with modern engines, unlimited processing power, online infrastructure, billions in revenue, and more cultural attention on HBCUs than at any point in recent memory — EA Sports said it was too complicated.

Technology improved by orders of magnitude.

The audience grew.

The cultural conversation around HBCUs grew.

And HBCU representation in college sports gaming went from full inclusion across multiple franchises to zero.

That is not an oversight. That is a series of decisions. Made year after year. By people who had the resources to do better and chose not to.

---

While EA was gone, and while EA came back without them, a community of fans refused to let the PS2 era die.

They built NCAA Next.

A free, open-source modification of NCAA Football 06 for PlayStation 2. Rebuilt for modern emulation. Updated rosters, new uniforms, current conference alignments, thousands of new textures.

Runs on your computer, your phone, your Steam Deck.

And because it's built on NCAA Football 06 — the last complete game, the one that had every HBCU — those programs are still in there.

You cannot get Grambling State in EA Sports College Football 26.

But you can fire up NCAA Next right now, today, for free — and Grambling is right where it always was.

That is what it looks like when the people who actually love this culture step up and do the work that a corporation decided wasn't worth doing.

---

This is bigger than nostalgia.

When people talk about the PS2 era with reverence — and they do, constantly, on forums and social media and comment sections — they're not just talking about the gameplay or the graphics or the dynasty modes.

They're talking about the feeling of full representation.

They're talking about a time when a kid in Louisiana could pick up a controller and play as Southern University. When a student at Howard could load up the basketball game and see their school competing at the same level as Duke and Kansas and Kentucky. When the entire ecosystem of college sports — not just the Power Five, not just the BCS, not just the programs ESPN covers every week — was inside the game.

The PS2 era was the only moment in the history of college sports gaming when that was true.

It has never been replicated.

And every year that EA puts out College Football without a single HBCU program, every year the TeamBuilder workaround gets offered as a substitute for real inclusion, is another year that a generation of fans gets told their schools don't matter enough to be in the game.

---

Here's where I leave you.

NCAA Football 06 on PlayStation 2 had Grambling, Jackson State, Florida A&M, Howard, NC A&T, FAMU, and eleven other HBCU programs. You could play as any of them.

College Hoops 2K8 had every Division I basketball program in the country, including every HBCU.

EA Sports College Football 26, in 2026, has none of them.

That is the entire story. That is what happened. That is what the golden age looked like, and that is what replaced it.

NCAA Next is keeping that era alive because the fans understand something the industry still hasn't figured out.

College sports gaming is not just about the Power Four conferences and the national title game.

It's about the Bayou Classic and the Florida Classic and Homecoming Saturday at Howard.

It's about the school that produced Walter Payton and the school that produced Shannon Sharpe and the school that produced Bob Hayes.

It's about the programs that existed in college football long before integration, that survived exclusion and neglect, that kept producing Hall of Famers and legends and stories that deserve to be told.

Those programs deserve to be in the game.

They were in the game.

And somebody made a decision to take them out.
